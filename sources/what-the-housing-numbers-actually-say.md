# What the Housing Numbers Actually Say — Summary

**Original source**: `references/devlinliles.com/what-the-housing-numbers-actually-say/what-the-housing-numbers-actually-say.md`

## Summary

The author takes the headline claim that a $39,000 house in 1975 now costs $420,000 and adjusts it for inflation and house size. In 2024 dollars that 1975 house was worth about $230,000, so the real gain is roughly 83 percent over 49 years (~1.25 percent per year); new homes also grew from 1,535 to 2,404 square feet, so real cost per square foot rose only about 17 percent. The genuine crisis, he argues, lives in a concentrated cluster of coastal markets and in two years of rate hikes, not in fifty years of uniform national drift.

## Key Claims

- Nominal prices rose ~10x, but inflation adjustment leaves a real increase of about 83 percent, or ~1.25 percent per year.
- Real price per square foot went from ~$150 in 1975 to $175 today, a 17 percent real rise; it peaked at $196 in 2022.
- Real median household income barely moved ($80,100 in 1975 vs. $80,610 in 2024), so the price-to-income ratio rose from 2.9x to 5.1x.
- About 76 percent of 234 tracked metros are below $450,000; removing the top 10 most expensive markets drops the mean from $396,000 to $362,000.
- The real crisis is payment burden: ~18 percent of gross income in 2019 rising to 31 percent by 2023.

## Relevance

A case study in reading statistics carefully and resisting narrative framing; useful for evidence-based decision-making and data literacy.

## Quality

Practitioner blog post by Devlin Liles. Densely sourced (Census/HUD, BLS CPI-U, NAR, Freddie Mac) with clear charts; it is an argued interpretation and the author acknowledges his own geographic bias.
