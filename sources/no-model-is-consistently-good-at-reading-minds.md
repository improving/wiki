# No Model Is Consistently Good at Reading Minds — Summary

**Original source**: `references/devlinliles.com/no-model-is-consistently-good-at-reading-minds/no-model-is-consistently-good-at-reading-minds.md`

## Summary

Researchers at the University of Maryland ran nine frontier vision-language models through two tasks borrowed from developmental psychology: a false-belief test and an animacy task that reads intention from moving shapes. No model led on both. The post argues that "theory of mind" is sold as one capability backed by one benchmark number, but the evidence shows it is a bundle of separable skills that do not transfer within a model.

## Key Claims

- A model strong on false-belief reasoning often stumbled on the animacy task, and the reverse held too; across nine models, no single one led on both.
- False-belief reasoning runs through language and chained inference; reading intention from motion is closer to perception, so strength in one buys no head start on the other.
- Marketing claims like "understands user intent" or "strong theory of mind" collapse a bundle of separable skills into one property.
- A single benchmark score speaks for whichever narrow slice that benchmark happened to test, and nothing more.
- The useful number is the gap between a model's two scores, a measure of how far a benchmark headline can be trusted.

## Relevance

Directly informs [Benchmark Validity](/concepts/benchmark-validity.md): a headline score describes one task, not a model-wide capability, so teams should ask which slice a deployed task actually draws on.
