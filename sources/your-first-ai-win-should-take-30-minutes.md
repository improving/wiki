# Your First AI Win Should Take 30 Minutes — Summary

**Original source**: `references/devlinliles.com/your-first-ai-win-should-take-30-minutes/your-first-ai-win-should-take-30-minutes.md`

## Summary

The post argues the biggest barrier to AI adoption is the wrong mental model of what starting looks like — a two-to-six-month on-ramp of understanding LLMs, taking prompt-engineering courses, choosing tools, and getting IT approval. Instead, it proposes a 30-minute experiment on a hated recurring task, judged by two questions (did it save time, did it feel better), then shared before it is polished. Drawing on Weick's "small wins" and Amabile's progress principle, it treats enjoyment as a legitimate success criterion alongside efficiency.

## Key Claims

- The imagined on-ramp costs two-to-six months before any feedback; none of it is necessary to begin.
- Karl Weick's 1984 "Small Wins" argues people freeze at full scale and progress comes from outcomes small enough to be concrete, complete, and implemented.
- The method: a hate list of ten most time-consuming recurring tasks (starred for tedium), a 30-minute unoptimized experiment, a two-question test, and sharing before polish.
- Teresa Amabile's progress principle holds that progress in meaningful work is the strongest motivator, so AI that removes tedium has value beyond minutes saved.
- The governance objection is met by constraining experiments (approved tools, no client or regulated data), not by a committee; sanctioned experiments are the governed alternative to shadow AI.

## Relevance

Informs [diffusion of innovation](/concepts/diffusion-of-innovations.md) as it applies to how AI adoption spreads from individual experiments to team capability.

## Quality

Practitioner coaching post by Devlin Liles. Prescriptive and opinion-led, backed by cited psychology (Weick, Amabile) but with no data on adoption or productivity outcomes.
