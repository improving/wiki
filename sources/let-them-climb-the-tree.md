# Let Them Climb the Tree — Summary

**Original source**: `references/driventodevelop.com/let-them-climb-the-tree/let-them-climb-the-tree.md`

## Summary

After his youngest tried something new and it went only mildly wrong, O'Hara noticed how instinctively he wanted to prevent it. He builds on an [Arthur Brooks](/entities/arthur-brooks.md) piece in [The Free Press](/entities/the-free-press.md) tracking Gen Z's pattern of retreat, and adds a thread he says Brooks leaves unpulled: the parents. We have built a parenting culture around preventing failure — intervening before the fall, smoothing the path, appealing to teachers and coaches. That sends the message that kids' failures are too big to handle and reflect on the parent, so they are never allowed to find out what they're made of.

## Key Claims

- In the mid-1970s, 92 percent of 12th graders had tried alcohol; by 2025 that had fallen to 47 percent — one signal in a broader pattern of retreat.
- Brooks' argument: the world is not actually more dangerous (divorce peaked in 1980, market volatility shows no upward trend, unemployment low) — the aversion to risk rose, not the risk.
- O'Hara's addition: parents, not just cultural pessimism, drive the retreat by protecting kids from consequences.
- Some helicopter parenting is about the parents — a child's struggle feels like the parent's report card.
- The fix is natural consequences: be there when the fall happens, sit with the difficulty, model processing failure, and ask "What did you learn?"

## Relevance

Links [resilience](/concepts/resilience.md) to parenting practice and to Brooks' call for optimism, arguing resilience is built through tolerated, low-stakes failure rather than protected.

## Quality

Practitioner blog post by David O'Hara. Well-argued and anchored in cited statistics from Brooks' piece; the parenting critique is opinion and single-case observation.
