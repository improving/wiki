# Vocabulary Is a Force Multiplier: Why Naming Things Precisely Matters — Summary

**Original source**: `references/devlinliles.com/vocabulary-is-a-force-multiplier-why-naming-things-precisely-matters/vocabulary-is-a-force-multiplier-why-naming-things-precisely-matters.md`

## Summary

Liles argues that giving something a precise name is a force multiplier: a term takes a concept that previously cost significant cognitive overhead to invoke and makes it nearly free, so a team can think about it, discuss it, and act on it. He illustrates the mechanism with the term "temporal coupling," catalogs the vocabulary central to his own work, and frames naming as a leadership obligation rather than a taxonomy hobby. He distinguishes precise vocabulary, which includes, from jargon, which excludes.

## Key Claims

- The naming mechanism: a recurring problem that must be re-explained every time becomes a three-second shorthand once it has a name, increasing the depth of thinking available to the team.
- The vocabulary he has coined or adopted: technical debt (from Ward Cunningham, sharpened to a chronic behavioral pattern), labor compression, demand-limited vs. collapsable tasks, stacking errors, Intent Era, and cognitive scaffolding.
- Each term was a response to a real gap — e.g., "labor compression" reframes AI's impact away from a binary job-replacement argument.
- Naming is a leadership act: it validates that a pattern is real, creates a shared reference point, and enables action rather than mere intuition.
- The inclusion test: does the person you explain it to say "yes, that — I've seen that," or look confused and never use it again?
- AI-native development vocabulary is early and noisy; terms like "AI integration," "AI adoption," "AI-assisted," and "AI-native" carry inconsistent meaning, so two organizations at the same nominal stage may mean different things.
- Practical starting point: when something important lacks a name, give it one, write it down, and use it consistently to see if it sticks.
