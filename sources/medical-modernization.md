# Medical Modernization — Summary

**Original source**: `references/improving.com/medical-modernization/medical-modernization.md`

## Summary

Improving's healthcare and life-sciences teams use Gartner's hype curve to explain why medical technology innovation is real but quiet, profiling companies at each phase from the plateau of productivity to the peak of inflated expectations. The through-line is that durable healthcare modernization depends on trust and long perseverance — mRNA vaccines rested on work begun in 1990 — not on the hype that surrounds it.

## Key Claims

- OzSystems reached the plateau of productivity: newborn screening and reporting technology plus regulatory mandates drastically reduced untreated early-childhood issues across many states and countries.
- On the slope of enlightenment, NextWave Connect builds secure communities where healthcare associations share best practices, and SETRAC lets Houston hospitals compare cardiac, stroke, trauma, and neonatal care data across systems.
- Telemedicine clients saw volume rise 5–15x during the pandemic, sparing stressed hospitals patient flow.
- mRNA vaccine work dates to 1990 mouse-cell experiments and 1992–93 theory, predating 2000s precision-medicine hype — perseverance, not hype, delivered it.
- Big players such as Johnson & Johnson, Optum, and Pfizer pour significant resources into healthcare innovation labs and data science.
- The Texas Medical Center, the world's largest medical city, has raised over $4 billion for cross-institutional life-sciences work.
- Trust, built on solid technology applied well, is what carries a technology across the hype curve into broad adoption.
