# Commitment Accounting: Promises Are Checks You Have to Cover — Summary

**Original source**: `references/devlinliles.com/commitment-accounting-promises-are-checks-you-have-to-cover/commitment-accounting-promises-are-checks-you-have-to-cover.md`

## Summary

Devlin Liles distills his most-given professional advice — "don't bounce a check" — into a model he calls [Commitment Accounting](/concepts/commitment-accounting.md). A commitment is a check drawn against your credibility account: the recipient builds plans and downstream promises around it, so missing it imposes a reputational cost far larger than the value of the original commitment. The essay covers the economics of trust, a progressive cost model for favors, how to renegotiate early, and why closing the loop deposits currency.

## Key Claims

- The reputational cost of a bounced check exceeds the value of the original commitment; the discipline is about the economics of trust, not perfectionism.
- Every subsequent ask to the same person costs more than the last, tilting the relationship toward extraction — the fix is to "earn between asks."
- The cost of renegotiating early (e.g. a Wednesday heads-up about a Friday deadline) is a fraction of the cost of missing it silently.
- In fast-moving organizations, commitment density is high and most failures are tracked informally, in people's memories and updated assessments of reliability.
- The most undervalued professional practice is closing the loop — confirming completion because it signals you track your commitments.
