# Skin in the Game: The Cost of Free — Summary

**Original source**: `references/driventodevelop.com/skin-in-the-game-the-cost-of-free/skin-in-the-game-the-cost-of-free.md`

## Summary

O'Hara examines the recurring "free or paid?" decision, arguing that free often costs more than expected because it lowers commitment. Drawing on years of hosting events and classes at Improving, he reports that free events see roughly 50% attendance relative to signups, while a small $20 fee lifts attendance to about 70% and higher prices approach 90%. The same logic applies to apps: paying creates a small promise that raises the chance of meaningful use. He notes the curve is non-linear — small prices sharply increase commitment, while higher prices shift the burden to demonstrating value — and advises pricing reasonably, then sticking to it.

## Key Claims

- Free events draw about 50% of signups; a $20 fee lifts that to roughly 70%.
- Charging more can push attendance close to 90% pull-through.
- Free lowers commitment; paying is a small promise to oneself that this matters.
- The price/commitment curve is non-linear, and higher prices demand clearly demonstrated value.
- Walking a price down later often ends in refunds and embarrassment; price reasonably and stick to it.

## Relevance

A consulting and leadership reflection on pricing, commitment, and value articulation, illustrated with [Improving](/entities/improving.md)'s own event history.

## Quality

Practitioner blog post by David O'Hara. Vivid and specific, but the attendance figures are from his own events and presented as broad-brush patterns rather than measured data.
