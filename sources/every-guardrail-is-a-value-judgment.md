# Every Guardrail Is a Value Judgment — Summary

**Original source**: `references/devlinliles.com/every-guardrail-is-a-value-judgment/every-guardrail-is-a-value-judgment.md`

## Summary

Deciding an AI should refuse some questions and answer others is epistemic paternalism — restricting information on the grounds it would harm the user — in tension with epistemic autonomy. Every content policy sits somewhere on a protection-versus-autonomy curve, whether or not its authors recognized the choice as philosophical. The post argues enterprises must justify each restriction explicitly, calibrate it to the specific user population, and review and audit it over time rather than shipping someone else's default.

## Key Claims

- Epistemic paternalism restricts access to knowledge for a person's own good; it directly tensions with the right to form one's own beliefs from one's own access to information.
- Neither extreme is defensible: a fully unrestricted system enables serious harm, and a maximally restricted one is less useful than a library and treats every user as a potential bad actor.
- A November 2025 Springer Nature paper on AI deskilling introduces "capacity-hostile environments," arguing excessive pre-filtering actively degrades users' capacity to reason independently.
- Three recurring failure modes: misplaced authority, bias amplification, and autonomy harm.
- "Maximize safety" treats the autonomy cost as zero; the right question is the tradeoff for this user population in this context.
- Guardrails need annual review and audit mechanisms, since an untested policy is epistemic paternalism rather than enforcement.
- Mill's harm principle, extended to informational harm, grounds but does not settle the contested extension.
