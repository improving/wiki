# Excel is a Cul-de-sac — Summary

**Original source**: `references/driventodevelop.com/excel-is-a-cul-de-sac/excel-is-a-cul-de-sac.md`

## Summary

O'Hara, newly reliant on Excel as a business tool rather than an occasional hack, weighs its low learning curve and ubiquity against its structural limits. His argument is not against Excel but against its overuse: spreadsheets passed around by email quietly become the operating system of significant companies. He lays out three "strikes" — non-reproducible manual manipulation, data that goes dead once transformed, and outputs that cannot be consumed by other systems — and concludes that when the need outgrows the tool, you must move out of your comfort zone and find something better.

## Key Claims

- Excel has a low learning curve and is widely known, so it becomes the default for running a business.
- Strike 1: transformations are almost always manual and rarely reproducible by anyone but the person who did them.
- Strike 2: manipulated data is "dead" — new data means starting from square one, not re-running the transformation.
- Strike 3: data cannot easily be consumed by other systems or fed back to the source without developer intervention, and chained spreadsheets form a "tangled and fragile web."
- The fault is overuse — the one-off that becomes permanent, the few users who become many — not Excel itself.

## Relevance

An early articulation of the [shadow IT](/concepts/shadow-it.md) pattern and of spreadsheet-driven [technical debt](/concepts/technical-debt.md); it also invokes [comfort zone](/concepts/comfort-zone.md) as the barrier to adopting proper tooling.

## Quality

Practitioner blog post by David O'Hara. Strong on concrete failure modes and memorable framing; the "three strikes" structure is the author's own analysis, and no data or case evidence is offered.
