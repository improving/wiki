# Dragging me to a camp — Summary

**Original source**: `references/driventodevelop.com/dragging-me-to-a-workout/dragging-me-to-a-workout.md`

## Summary

A longtime 5 a.m. gym regular, O'Hara reluctantly agrees to a one-month trial of Camp Gladiator after his wife connects with a former training partner who leads a group in Frisco, Texas. The outdoor workouts — and especially the people — win him over, and he stays. He uses the switch to argue that comfortable, known routines can masquerade as growth, and that a sense of community is what actually keeps people showing up.

## Key Claims

- The author's prior routine: at the gym door at 5 a.m., headphones on, working out alone.
- He committed only to one month of Camp Gladiator, intending to return to the gym afterward.
- The workouts are outdoors in a park parking lot, in cold, wind, or Texas heat — a stark change from the gym.
- He credits the group's diversity (elite athletes, middle-of-the-pack members, and those just trying to move) and its banter with creating community.
- His conclusion: "we can get our ruts and think we're growing in the comfortableness of routine" when what is needed is a different experience.

## Relevance

Touches [comfort zone](/concepts/comfort-zone.md) directly, and is a case study in how belonging and [social capital](/concepts/social-capital.md) drive sustained behavior change.

## Quality

Practitioner blog post by David O'Hara. Light, first-person narrative with no data; its value is anecdotal illustration of routine, community, and behavior change rather than generalizable evidence.
