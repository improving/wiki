# Tech Education — Summary

**Original source**: `references/driventodevelop.com/tech-education/tech-education.md`

## Summary

Writing as a dropout, O'Hara argues that school is good but broken, and that tech education is disconnected from a fast-moving industry. Universities prioritize theory and outdated practices, treating students as products molded to academic benchmarks rather than professionals entering a dynamic market. He positions consulting firms as key bridge-builders: unlike tenured professors who may lag industry trends, consultants are motivated to stay current and bring real-world experience. He recommends internships, certifications, and regularly updated curricula developed jointly by educators and industry professionals, arguing the payoff is more job-ready graduates, a better-skilled talent pool for companies, and a more responsive educational system.

## Key Claims

- Traditional tech education emphasizes theory and outdated practices, leaving graduates ill-prepared for industry work.
- Universities often treat students as products for academic benchmarks rather than professionals entering a market.
- Consultants, motivated to stay relevant, are better positioned than some tenured professors to modernize curricula.
- Internships, certifications, and industry-informed curriculum updates are the practical levers.
- Closing the gap benefits students (job readiness), companies (relevant skills, productivity, innovation), and the system itself.
