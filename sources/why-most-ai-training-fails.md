# Why Most AI Training Fails — Summary

**Original source**: `references/improving.com/why-most-ai-training-fails/why-most-ai-training-fails.md`

## Summary

Blake McMillan argues most AI training fails because it teaches history, glossaries, and prompt libraries that don't connect to the work people actually do. AI underdelivers not because the technology is broken but because nobody taught people a different way to interact with it — how to structure context, iterate, and verify. The post cites adoption-versus-training gaps and argues that the durable skills are interaction habits, not tool knowledge, which is why hands-on practice tied to real tasks beats demos and video courses.

## Key Claims

- McKinsey's 2025 survey found 78% of organizations use AI in at least one function, but a WalkMe study reported only 7.5% of employees had extensive AI training, and ManpowerGroup found 56% of workers globally received none.
- The number-one concern for new users is trust — "I don't know if it's just going to lie to me" — and training either ignores it or explains hallucinations without showing what to do.
- The fix is not asking people to trust AI but teaching them to challenge it: ask where a claim came from, request direct quotes, and surface assumptions.
- Treating AI like a search engine fails; the first response is rarely the one to keep, and more context yields better output.
- Waiting to learn AI is a mistake; Gallup found 45% of US employees now use AI at work, nearly doubling from 21% in 2023, and EY found companies leave up to 40% of productivity gains on the table due to talent-strategy gaps.
- DataCamp's 2026 report found 82% of leaders provide AI training yet 59% still report a skills gap, with video-based courses the weakest format.
- Three skills matter most: uncovering insights from messy inputs, generating ideas past the blank page (ask for multiple options, then iterate), and drafting communication with accountability — AI can draft but does not send.
- Fundamentals defined as interaction discipline are not tied to any model or release cycle; the gap is between occasional and reliable use.
