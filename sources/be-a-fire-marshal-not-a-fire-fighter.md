# Be a Fire Marshal, not a Fire Fighter — Summary

**Original source**: `references/driventodevelop.com/be-a-fire-marshal-not-a-fire-fighter/be-a-fire-marshal-not-a-fire-fighter.md`

## Summary

Leaders are usually cast as firefighters, constantly solving problems and averting crises. The author argues the better role is fire marshal: preventing the fires rather than fighting them. He lays out four shifts — proactive over reactive, strategic over tactical, big picture over small details, and identifying danger zones before problems ignite. The through-line is that prevention is deliberate work that must be scheduled and practiced, not squeezed in between crises.

## Key Claims

- The best leaders stay one step ahead, anticipating problems and taking steps to avoid them; purely reactive leadership leads to burnout.
- Strategy is long-term and taxing, so leaders must intentionally set aside dedicated time for it.
- Delegating and trusting the team prevents micromanagement, frustration, and burnout; accountability structures and follow-up keep confidence that work gets done.
- Great leaders, like firefighters who know where fires start, understand where problems are likely to arise and can avoid or mitigate them — saving time, money, and stress.

## Relevance

Centers on [delegation](/concepts/delegation.md) as the mechanism for escaping tactical, detail-level work, and frames proactive leadership as a practice that must be scheduled.
