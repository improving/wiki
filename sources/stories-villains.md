# Stories & Villains — Summary

**Original source**: `references/driventodevelop.com/stories-villains/stories-villains.md`

## Summary

Starting from the line "there's a villain in every story," O'Hara observes that no one thinks they are that villain: we cast ourselves as the hero, with good reasons for everything we do. Breaking out of that self-talk is hard — we prefer to be the flawed hero than to admit we caused the problem — so we explain away wreckage, get defensive, or avoid the truth. His prescription is simple but uncomfortable: sometimes say "I'm sorry," and step outside your own story to see the mess from someone else's point of view. That shift, he argues, is the real growth.

## Key Claims

- Everyone is the hero of their own story and rarely sees themselves as the villain.
- Self-talk and defensiveness protect us from admitting we caused problems.
- Most people aren't out to sabotage; real evil exists but is rare and concentrated.
- Sometimes the honest answer is simply to apologize.
- Seeing a situation from another's point of view is the growth that stops you being the villain, even unintentionally.
