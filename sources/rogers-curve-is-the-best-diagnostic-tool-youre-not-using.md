# Rogers' Curve Is the Best Diagnostic Tool You're Not Using — Summary

**Original source**: `references/devlinliles.com/rogers-curve-is-the-best-diagnostic-tool-youre-not-using/rogers-curve-is-the-best-diagnostic-tool-youre-not-using.md`

## Summary

Devlin Liles has applied Everett Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations across three technology eras and argues it is a diagnostic rather than a prediction: it describes how a population is distributed across adoption stages at a moment, not when a practice will arrive. Mapping your organization's position against the market's — and the direction of the gap — determines strategy. He walks through 2013, 2018, and 2025 mappings and warns against applying a stage's strategy you do not occupy.

## Key Claims

- The curve's segments are Innovators (2.5%), Early Adopters (13.5%), Early Majority (34%), Late Majority (34%), and Laggards (16%).
- 2013: source control sat at Late Majority/Laggard, unit testing and CI at Early Majority, infrastructure as code and QA automation at Early Adopter, and containerization at Innovator (Docker shipped that year).
- 2018: CI had moved to Late Majority and containerization to Early Majority; the limiting factor had become organizational trust rather than tooling.
- 2025: individual GenAI tools (Copilot, ChatGPT) are Early-to-Late Majority, AI workflow integration is Early Adopter, and autonomous agents are Innovator.
- The common mistake is applying an Innovator's strategy while actually sitting in the Late Majority, or the reverse.
