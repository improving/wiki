# Same Model, Different Outcome — Who's Responsible? — Summary

**Original source**: `references/devlinliles.com/same-model-different-outcome-whos-responsible/same-model-different-outcome-whos-responsible.md`

## Summary

The post imports the philosophical concept of moral luck — Nagel and Williams, 1979 — into the AI liability debate. A model behaving exactly as trained can produce catastrophic harm when fed an adversarial prompt, and the causal chain from developer to deployer to user to attacker has uneven link lengths. The author argues that EU strict-liability rules oversimplify this, and that enterprise deployers carry more responsibility than they typically acknowledge.

## Key Claims

- Thomas Nagel and Bernard Williams (1979) argued we routinely judge people on outcomes partially outside their control, a tension with no clean solution.
- Directive (EU) 2024/2853, published November 2024, replaces the 1985 product liability framework, includes software and AI systems as "product," and establishes strict liability for AI-caused physical or property damage.
- Anchoring liability to the developer simplifies the legal question but does not resolve the moral one, since four parties made choices.
- Deployers carry more liability than "we're just using a vendor model" suggests, because they chose the model, context, access policy, and monitoring.
- Monitoring converts moral luck into moral agency, and incident response documentation answers the reasonable-care question courts and regulators ask.
