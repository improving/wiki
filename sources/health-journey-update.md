# Health Journey: Update — Summary

**Original source**: `references/driventodevelop.com/health-journey-update/health-journey-update.md`

## Summary

Drawing on [Outlive](/entities/outlive.md) by [Peter Attia](/entities/peter-attia.md), O'Hara argues that biology is complex and individual, so one-size-fits-all diets and superfoods deserve skepticism — especially when someone is selling them. He prefers numbers over subjective "do I feel better" judgments, citing the time he and his wife ate the same meals on the [Levels](/entities/levels.md) CGM and had noticeably different glucose responses. His protocol uses the six-month baseline from [Function Health](/entities/function-health.md), layered with intermediate testing when tuning specific measures.

## Key Claims

- One-size-fits-all diets, superfoods, and exercise programs are often sold rather than proven.
- Approach experimentation safely, scientifically, and with clear rational thought.
- His own CGM comparison with his wife showed different bodily responses to the same meals.
- He trusts data over subjective measures like "do I feel better."
- The Function Health six-month baseline anchors his protocols, with intermediate testing layered on.

## Relevance

Frames the Health Journey series' philosophy: data-driven, individualized [continuous glucose monitoring](/concepts/continuous-glucose-monitoring.md) and [biomarker testing](/concepts/biomarker-testing.md) over generic advice.

## Quality

Practitioner blog post by David O'Hara. Opinion and personal experience rather than evidence; the shared-meal CGM comparison is anecdotal.
