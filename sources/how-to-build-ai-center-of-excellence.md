# How to Build an AI Center of Excellence — Summary

**Original source**: `references/improving.com/how-to-build-ai-center-of-excellence/how-to-build-ai-center-of-excellence.md`

## Summary

An AI Center of Excellence decides how AI gets built and governed across an organization, and its first job is sorting AI practices into three tiers: centrally mandatory, a shared default teams can deviate from, and fully local to each team. The post argues this split matters more than the org chart, and that governance must participate during the work through quality gates rather than auditing after the fact.

## Key Claims

- The three tiers are "always true, centrally managed" (e.g., a PCI compliance check no team should customize), "shared definition framework" (templates and rubrics teams may adjust with reason), and "only true for one team, fully democratized" (e.g., a Java coding-standard agent irrelevant to an SAP team).
- Without a CoE, organizations get either fragmented practice (five teams building five versions of the same compliance check) or a governance binder nobody reads.
- A global industrial automation company built a CoE on a custom architecture with no tier distinction and was still stuck on a single document/RFP workflow nine or ten months in.
- Roles: an engagement/program lead who maps readiness before rollout, a data steward/compliance owner scoped to the mandatory tier, an embedded technical role working real backlog, and a change/enablement lead (the most understaffed role).
- Identity is the real failure mechanism: a tester whose identity is "I run these scripts" experiences an AI test-generation agent as a threat, while one who "owns quality" experiences it as leverage; frame the change as working at the top of one's license.
- SOP governance fails at velocity: a quarterly audit of a 200-outputs-per-week agent accumulates roughly 2,600 documents before review, so quality gates that check output as it is produced catch drift in week two instead of week fourteen.
- First 90 days: two to three weeks mapping team readiness, a documented three-tier split, one pilot cohort on real backlog, and an identity conversation run by the change lead. Start with four people.
