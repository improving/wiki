# It's Simple, Right? — Summary

**Original source**: `references/driventodevelop.com/its-simple-right/its-simple-right.md`

## Summary

O'Hara argues that we oversimplify what we don't deeply understand: the further we sit from the details, the simpler the solution looks, and we fill gaps with assumptions, mistaking omission for intent and seeing malice where there was only a blind spot. The cost is both misunderstanding others and missing our own chance to grow. He offers a corrective practice of pausing to ask "What don't I know?" and, at [Improving](/entities/improving.md), the sharper question: "Why would a perfectly rational, reasonable human being do this if they wanted to be successful?"

## Key Claims

- Oversimplification leads us to write unhelpful stories about people, assuming intent where there was only a blind spot.
- The "rational human" question shifts the frame toward positive intent, assuming people want to succeed rather than fail.
- The shift works in two ways: it keeps you grounded in curiosity rather than judgment, and it returns the burden of effort to what you control.
- Even without agreement, the exercise builds understanding and often reveals a path you couldn't see before.
- Pro tip: if a sentence starts with "If you just...", you're probably oversimplifying.

## Relevance

A short practitioner argument for [empathy](/concepts/empathy.md) as a deliberate mental discipline rather than a personality trait, tied to leading with clarity and strength.

## Quality

Practitioner blog post by David O'Hara. Concise and persuasive; evidence is anecdotal and the argument is a heuristic, not an empirical claim.
