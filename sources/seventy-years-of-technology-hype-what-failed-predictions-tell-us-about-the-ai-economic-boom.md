# 70 Years of Tech Hype: What Failed Predictions Tell Us About the AI Economic Boom — Summary

**Original source**: `references/devlinliles.com/seventy-years-of-technology-hype-what-failed-predictions-tell-us-about-the-ai-economic-boom/seventy-years-of-technology-hype-what-failed-predictions-tell-us-about-the-ai-economic-boom.md`

## Summary

The post surveys confident, quantified technology forecasts from 1950 onward — the leisure society, the paperless office, Rifkin's end of work, the dot-com new economy, Frey and Osborne's 47% — and finds they failed in the same way: overestimating speed and confusing "technically possible" with economically inevitable. It argues the 2023–2025 AI forecasts from Goldman Sachs, McKinsey, PwC, and the IMF share those structural flaws, and recommends planning against historical base rates.

## Key Claims

- The same five errors recur: conflating task feasibility with economy-wide deployment, ignoring organizational lag (the Solow paradox), underestimating Jevons-induced demand, assuming automatic productivity gains, and credentialed forecasters with commercial stakes.
- The paperless office failed on its own terms: worldwide office paper use more than doubled from 1980 to 2000.
- Goldman Sachs (March 2023) projected +7% global GDP, +1.5pp annual productivity, and 300 million FTE jobs exposed; PwC projected $15.7 trillion by 2030.
- Daron Acemoglu (2024) models a 1.1–1.6% GDP gain over a decade — about one-fifth of Goldman's — with only ~5% of tasks profitably automated.
- MIT Project NANDA (July 2025) found 95% of enterprise GenAI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact despite $30–40 billion in spending.
- The author recommends a base case of 0.5–1% added annual productivity and treating consensus forecasts as falsified if pilot-to-production conversion stays under 10–15% by 2027.
