# Effective Communication

Effective communication is the deliberate practice of conveying intent clearly and confirming that it landed, rather than broadcasting information and assuming it was received. Improving's writing treats it as repeated, contextual, and two-way — the skill that underpins trust, alignment, and performance, and the one most strained by remote and cross-cultural work. It depends less on eloquence than on listening, honesty, and checking shared understanding.

## Key Claims

- Three myths to debunk: that communication means telling everything, that saying it once suffices, and that delivered information is automatically actionable — "a lack of communication does not quell concerns; it feeds paranoia."
- Share big-picture information often and be explicit about whether you are informing or seeking feedback; reach large groups through repeated messaging across channels.
- Albert Mehrabian's model attributes roughly 55% of communication to body language and 38% to tone, leaving 7% to words — so virtual work loses most non-verbal signal and alignment must be built deliberately.
- Agreement is not alignment: people can nod along without shared interpretation, and [Patrick Lencioni](/entities/patrick-lencioni.md)'s five dysfunctions trace the silence to an absent trust and a fear of conflict.
- "Clarify Expectations" (from *The Speed of Trust*) means reflecting back what you understood and asking "Is this correct?" — clear is kind, even when it reveals a poor fit.
- Active listening — listen without judgment, without preparing to respond — makes people feel heard and valued, deepens understanding, and builds trust.
- Cross-cultural communication requires attention to language, habits, and the clash between hierarchical and egalitarian norms.
