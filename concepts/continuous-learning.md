# Continuous Learning

Continuous learning treats skill growth as an ongoing habit rather than a series of one-off courses, so that people and teams keep pace as technology and ways of working shift. Improving's writing frames it as a culture — employees teaching employees, learning embedded in daily work, and outcomes measured by changed behavior rather than certificates earned. The payoff is that teams use change as a competitive advantage instead of merely keeping up.

## Key Claims

- [ImprovingU](/entities/improvingu.md) lets any employee teach a class on a specialty, on the belief that "everyone is the smartest person in the room" when their topic comes up — and that teaching sharpens the teacher too.
- Don McGreal's "four D's" critique holds that traditional instructor-led training is disruptive, dense, detached, and disregarded; certifications create a baseline but "a driver's license doesn't make you a good driver."
- Deeper Learning Programs spread 30–50 objectives across six to ten weeks with real-world assignments, turning abstract knowledge into lived experience; labs, fieldwork, and coaching anchor the rest.
- Learning modality matters: in-person wins on feedback, focus, and relationships, live virtual adds flexibility, and self-paced is cheap but loses real-time collaboration — the payoff is skills used daily.
- A learning strategy should be measured in a three-tier stack: activity metrics (completion, retention), performance metrics (observable behavior change), and business outcomes (ROI, client satisfaction, project success).
- Blake McMillan argues AI training fails when it teaches glossaries and prompt libraries instead of interaction habits — context, iteration, and verification — tied to real tasks.
- Adoption outpaces training: one cited survey found 78% of organizations using AI but only 7.5% of employees with extensive AI training.
