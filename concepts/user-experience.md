# User Experience

User experience (UX) is the whole of how someone experiences a product or service, not merely its visual interface — the term Don Norman coined in the early 1990s at Apple. Improving's UX writers argue it is everyone's responsibility, not the preserve of designers, because products that fail human expectations fail the companies behind them. Done well, UX pairs strategy with what is valuable to users and the organization, and is measured by behavior change rather than artifacts.

## Key Claims

- Common misconceptions reduce UX to aesthetics or interfaces, ignoring its impact on functionality, satisfaction, and business success.
- The field's lineage runs from the Bauhaus (1919, fusing art, craft, and technology) and the first industrial design program at Carnegie Mellon (1934) through the internet and mobile shifts to dynamic, interactive experiences.
- Terminology has shifted toward "product design," reflecting that everyone builds, maintains, and is part of the product pipeline.
- The UX mindset means fail fast, empathy, active listening, and human-centered design — falling in love with the problem rather than the solution.
- UX integrates best into product teams through adaptable shared principles — short cycles, retrospectives, go-and-see, testing high-risk hypotheses, radical transparency, and learning as a first-class backlog item — rather than a fixed rulebook.
- Designing well means dropping ego, asking better questions (dig past surface requests to the underlying "why"), noticing UX everywhere, and breaking rules deliberately after mastering the building blocks.
- Accessibility is part of the experience: assistive technologies only work if sites cooperate, and accessible design improves SEO and prepares for regulation — nearly 5% of the US population has some color blindness.
- A design-led approach defines success metrics up front and prioritizes outcomes over outputs.
