# AI maturity model

An AI maturity model describes how much authority an organization can responsibly delegate to AI, and what infrastructure each level demands. The claim across this corpus is that maturity is a measure of *capability handed off*, not of how good the tools are — and that most organizations overestimate where they stand because they measure activity instead of capability.

## Key Claims

- Five major frameworks (BCG, Gartner, Bain, McKinsey, NIST) describe slices of one shape but none tells an organization which stage it occupies; Improving's eight-stage model supplies the missing ruler.
- Every stage has concrete proof criteria — demonstrable artifacts, not subjective feelings — and most organizations are at Stage 2 while believing they are at Stage 4.
- Stage 2 gains are personal and ephemeral: assistants speed individuals but nothing is encoded or shared, so the team cannot compound learning.
- Stage 3 encodes one person's judgment into personal agents and shifts the role from producing to directing and reviewing; a healthy acceptance rate runs roughly 92–94%.
- Coding benchmarks overstate readiness: on FrontierCode, Claude Opus 4.8 scored 13.4% on the hardest merge-ready tasks, and SaaSBench found over 95% of agent failures happen before business logic.
- Gains attenuate up the stack: across 100,000+ developers, commits rose 180% but released software only 30%, with consumer usage flat.
- Maturity is visible at the regional level too: a 700-person DFW AI awards night and an AI 75 list led by operators and institutions rather than startups mark a market that has moved from hype to institutional infrastructure ([when 700 people show up](/sources/when-700-people-show-up-for-an-ai-awards-night-the-hype-is-over.md)).
- That shift changes the question buyers ask — from whether to do AI to how well it is being done — and buying decisions now form among institutions already deploying rather than among first movers.
