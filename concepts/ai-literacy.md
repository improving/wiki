# AI literacy

AI literacy is the working understanding of AI capabilities and limits that lets people use and question it well. The sources argue that most training fails because it teaches history, glossaries, and prompt libraries disconnected from real work, and that the durable skills are interaction habits — structuring context, iterating, and verifying — not tool knowledge. The number-one concern of new users is trust, so literacy means learning to challenge AI rather than to trust it.

## Key Claims

- McKinsey's 2025 survey found 78% of organizations use AI in at least one function, but only 7.5% of employees had extensive training and 56% of workers globally received none.
- DataCamp's 2026 report found 82% of leaders provide AI training yet 59% still report a skills gap, with video-based courses the weakest format.
- The core technique is challenging AI: ask where a claim came from, request direct quotes, and surface assumptions rather than asking people to trust it.
- Treating AI like a search engine fails; the first response is rarely the one to keep, and more context yields better output.
- Three skills matter most: uncovering insights from messy inputs, generating ideas past the blank page, and drafting communication with accountability — AI can draft but does not send.
- Fundamental terms need untangling: AI is an umbrella term; data science, big data, advanced analytics, and machine learning are distinct; deep learning is a neural net with more than three layers.
- Applied education works: Improving's AI Summer Camp taught Houston students and parents what AI is and isn't, and to use it as a thought partner rather than a replacement.
