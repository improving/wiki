# blockchain

A blockchain is a growing chain of cryptographically linked transaction blocks, replicated across many nodes as a distributed digital ledger. Because altering any past block would require rebuilding every later one, the ledger resists tampering and can be verified independently. In this corpus its core benefit is framed as acting as an automated trusted third party for parties that do not trust each other, rather than as a currency.

## Key Claims

- A blockchain is a distributed, append-only ledger; blocks link by cryptography so past records are resistant to change and verifiable.
- It is not digital money: cryptocurrency is one implementation built on a blockchain, like a website on web technologies.
- It is not anonymous: the ledger is distributed and verifiable by design, and services like Whale Alert de-anonymize transactions in real time.
- Storing bulk data on-chain slows the calculations that tie blocks together, so blocks typically hold minimal references to external systems.
- Its value is disintermediation — the house-closing example removes the title company — lowering transaction costs and increasing economic flow.
- Consensus (proof of work vs. proof of stake) is attackable via 51% or 34% attacks; some chains combine methods to trade off speed and security.
- Banking (mortgages, title verification), supply chains (sourcing visibility), and provenance (art, equities, real estate) stand to benefit soonest.
- It was cited as a growth market at $5.92 billion in 2021, forecast to grow at an 85.9% CAGR, driven by immutability, security, transparency, and decentralization.
