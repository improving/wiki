# digital accessibility

Digital accessibility is the practice of making products usable by people with disabilities, and under the Americans with Disabilities Act it is also a legal requirement for government-audited and public-sector products. Improving writers frame it as both a design responsibility and a compliance matter, defined by the WCAG A/AA/AAA levels and enforced through regulation and litigation. It matters because disabled users are a large audience — and because accessible design improves the experience, searchability, and reach of products for everyone.

## Key Claims

- Under ADA Title II, public entities serving at least 50,000 people must make their websites comply with WCAG 2.1 Level AA; the original April 24, 2026 deadline was extended by the DOJ to April 26, 2027.
- WCAG defines levels A/AA/AAA, with AA the minimum for products (except decorative messaging) and AAA the goal in some cases; WCAG 2.1 aims for a perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust experience.
- Four foundational practices: keyboard navigation as a primary function, proper form labeling with a vertical layout, alternative text for informative images, and accessible color contrast and font sizing.
- According to the CDC, more than 1 in 4 US adults live with a disability and globally the figure exceeds one billion; nearly 5% of the US population has color blindness and 3.6% has a visual impairment.
- Penalties can include daily fines per infraction, and private digital-accessibility lawsuits are increasing, using WCAG 2.1 as their benchmark.
- Accessibility should be added to the Definition of Done; suggested actions include a "No Mouse Monday" keyboard test, a UI scan, a screen-reader walkthrough, and a vision-impairment simulation.
- A Disney World case shows what anticipation looks like: over 50 rides with wheelchair access, a Disability Access Service return-time pass, and dedicated first-aid rooms.
