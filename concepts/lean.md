# Lean

Lean is the discipline of eliminating waste and maximizing the flow of value, inherited from the Toyota Production System. In this corpus Lean is presented as the natural companion to Agile: Agile breaks work into small increments to reduce complexity and risk, while Lean focuses on efficiency at the pull of the customer by removing waste, errors, and delays.

## Key Claims

- The seven Lean principles: eliminate waste, build quality in, create knowledge, defer commitment, deliver fast, respect people, and optimize the whole ([Go Lean or Go Home](/sources/go-lean-or-go-home.md)).
- Eliminating waste means watching for Muda (wastefulness), Mura (imbalance), and Muri (overload) — examples include extra approvals and refining backlog items more than one to two sprints ahead ([Go Lean or Go Home](/sources/go-lean-or-go-home.md)).
- Build quality in uses early-and-often quality, five whys and Ishikawa diagrams for root cause, and poka yoke to mistake-proof product and process; create knowledge happens in retrospectives via Hansei and Kaizen ([Go Lean or Go Home](/sources/go-lean-or-go-home.md)).
- Defer commitment means deciding at the last possible moment and architecting for flexibility so fewer increments are irreversible ([Go Lean or Go Home](/sources/go-lean-or-go-home.md)).
- Lean's 14 Toyota principles align closely with the Agile Manifesto's four values and twelve principles; tools without values are unsatisfying and unfulfilling ([The Value of Values](/sources/the-value-of-values.md)).
- Lean thinking applied to team structure removes the coordination waste of non-cross-functional teams, which otherwise choose delays and dependencies ([What You Don't Change, You Choose](/sources/what-you-dont-change-you-choose.md)).
- Lean thinking, alongside systems thinking, underpins "agile at scale" — refining how work flows from concept to cash ([Agility: to Scale or Not to Scale](/sources/agile-to-scale-or-not-to-scale.md)).
