# design thinking

Design thinking is an iterative, human-centered way to frame problems and shape solutions, cycling on real user problems rather than locking in a plan. Improving writers compare it to two sibling methodologies — Agile responds to change through short sprints, and Lean Startup builds only what is necessary around an MVP — and argue no single methodology fits every context. It matters because teams that empower themselves with design thinking's empathy, ideation, and purposeful iteration surface opportunities and anticipate challenges earlier.

## Key Claims

- Design thinking, Agile, and Lean Startup are complementary routes to the same goal — solving real user problems — not competing doctrines.
- Empathy, ideation, and purposeful iteration are the core moves, applied to complex problems to distill them into actionable solutions.
- Design sprints and similar practices keep user and business results central rather than artifact production.
- Quoting Jared Spool, teams should fall in love with the problem rather than the solution, then iterate with research.
- Ten integration principles for embedding the approach in product teams include working in short cycles, go-and-see (gemba walks), testing high-risk hypotheses, doing less research more often, and making learning a first-class backlog item.
- Design thinking is used at project kickoff to align goals and scope, and in service of outcomes — every artifact should link to an intended result.
