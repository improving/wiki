Dartmouth just renamed their Center for Professional Development to the Center for Career Design, and is now "shifting its focus to helping students find their areas of passion rather than a direct focus on one profession." I read that and had to put my phone down.

No. A thousand times no.

I’ve written before about why “[follow your passion” is bad advice](https://driventodevelop.com/2017/04/23/screw-finding-your-passion/) (the research has been clear on this for years), and we keep doing it anyway. [The article that surfaced this gem](https://www.businessinsider.com/employers-colleges-gen-z-workplace-readiness-2026-8?ref=driventodevelop.com) is actually about something real and worth solving: employers are frustrated that new graduates can't communicate, can't navigate professional relationships, can't sit through a difficult conversation without crumbling. That’s a solvable problem. Skills can be taught. But Dartmouth’s response is to help students feel their way into the right career. That’s not a solution. That’s an accelerant of the wrong kind.

Here is what the research actually shows: [we are remarkably bad at predicting what will make us happy](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00355.x?ref=driventodevelop.com). What we call passion is largely a feeling, and feelings are tied to novelty and early success. When the work gets hard (and it will), or when the results don't match the timing or outcomes we envisioned, the obvious conclusion is that [we picked the wrong passion](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797618780643?ref=driventodevelop.com). So we start over. We look for a different feeling. We meander. We blame. And then we wonder why an entire generation is struggling to get traction.

Passion is not the input. It's the output. It comes as a byproduct of getting really good at something. The market doesn't pay for feelings. It pays for competence, reliability, and craft. Find the thing you have an aptitude for, then invest everything you have into becoming better at it than the people around you. That's what builds a career. That's when passion shows up.

Colleges can absolutely help students get there. Teach them to communicate. Teach them to fail and recover. Teach them to sit in discomfort without escaping. Help them discover what they're actually good at through real work, not introspection. That's career development.

What are you telling the young people in your life to chase?
